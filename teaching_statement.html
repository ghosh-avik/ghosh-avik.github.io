Teaching and mentorship

I have been a teaching assistant for three courses at UC San Diego MAE, totaling more than 500 enrolled students, covering control, instrumentation and thermal fundamentals. On the research side, I am currently mentoring an ECE MS student in my postdoc lab, and leading a group of former colleagues from my PhD lab exploring extensions of my past works.

Teaching philosophy

The moments I remember best from my own education are the aha ones — when a conversation with a teacher or a collaborator pushed me to see the broader structure behind a specific idea, or just opened up a question I had never thought to ask. Reading an elegant mathematical proof, I always ask what the thinking process must have been that produced its main idea. That process is usually hidden behind the mathematical machinery, and is the genuinely transferable skill. I get a kick out of setting up those moments for someone else. So in office hours, discussion sessions, and with the students I mentor, I ask them to walk me through their own reasoning, and then work back with them to the idea underneath, rather than simply explaining at them. It is a large part of why I want to spend my life in academia.

With the students I teach and mentor, I focus on three things. First, a rigorous command of the mathematical tools of design and analysis — understanding why a method works, not just how to run it. Second, formulating technically sound research questions that take their environmental, ethical and social context seriously. Third, audience-driven communication: the same result must be told differently to a control theorist, a microgrid operator, and a policymaker, and learning to make that switch is part of doing science. In helping students develop these skills, I sharpen my own.
